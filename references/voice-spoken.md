## How Mike explains things

- Opens with earned credibility, plainly. "Over 40 years", "doing agile for about 15 years". No bragging, just context.
- States what he noticed, then why it matters. "What I noticed was..." then the consequence.
- Uses one concrete example per point. A bug fix that silently fixed something else. A day of tokens gone in five hours.
- Cause and effect, in order. No gates, so drift, so cost.
- Gives numbers when he has them. One story per day. $20 plan. 120 stories.
- Hedges honestly. "I haven't proven it yet." Says what he doesn't know.
- Strong opinions, said calmly. "I would discourage them." "I don't see any other way of doing it."
- Tells a short story to land a point (the Ford auditorium story).
- Turns it back to the business. The question is always what the business needs.
- Uses roles and gates as the subject, not himself. "The reviewer has to look at it in a separate session." Matches the memory rule: roles and gates drive the work.

## Best lines (lightly cleaned, usable as tone anchors)

> I've been working in the IT industry over 40 years. I've done everything from coder to agile coach to scrum master to project management.

> What I've noticed, especially in the agile world, is people skip parts of the ceremonies. Their reasoning is that it's too cumbersome, too time consuming. When you skip any part of it, the product that comes out is not as strong. Lots of defects and a lot of rework.

> I needed to treat AI in the SDLC process as if it was its own agile process. In agile you have a feedback mechanism at the end of each sprint.

> If you don't have the gates, it's going to drift, and it's going to cost a lot more money than you expect.

> Before the gates, I could go through a day's worth of tokens in five or six hours. Now I don't run out of tokens at all.

> I'd say "fix this bug." And while it was looking at that code, it would say "oh, I can fix this too." And it would silently fix it. So I had to have a guard that says, only fix what I tell you to fix.

> Just because the code says it's done, the reviewer has to look at it in a separate session. It doesn't know anything about what the coder did. So there's no drift.

> If you're not doing that feedback loop into your AI, you're not taking advantage of what AI does for you, because it's not learning anything.

> I think AI is now the outsourcing for developers. If the developers don't understand the business they're in, they're going to be out of a job.

> All of the manual work that people say is complex is only complex because we make it complex.

> I will bet money that when you were there, there were no documented SLAs.

## Spoken tics to cut in writing

- "um", "you know", "I mean", false starts, restarts mid-sentence
- "At the end of the day" more than once
- Stacked "So, so, so" openers
- Run-ons that chain four ideas. Split them. One idea per sentence.
- "Basically", "kind of", "pretty much" as padding
